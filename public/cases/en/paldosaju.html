About the project

Paldo Saju is an AI saju consultation service from my studio, Raymind.AI. Saju is Korean four pillars fortune reading. Gifted masters from all eight provinces of Korea, the paldo in the name, hear the user's story first, then talk them through the saju drawn from their birthday.

I am building it on my own. I do the planning, screen design and metric design myself, and around 30 AI agents share the operations, each with its own role. Agents for priorities, deployment, finance, data, the funnel, pricing and ads each work within set permissions, and every change goes into the change log. So that a small team could move like a large organisation, I designed the roles, permissions and decision criteria first.

00Product

One worry and a birthday, and a master talks it through with you.

Saju is full of difficult terms and dense information. Paldo Saju keeps the saju chart small at the top, and the master talks it through in conversation, shaped around the worry the user picked. The structure starts from the user's question and brings out only as much of the complex information as they need. The free reading leads into the reading contents and then to the checkout screen, and users pay straight away, with no app to install and no login.

Screens are in Korean.

01The user problem I set out to solve

Users heard the free reading to the end, then stopped at the checkout screen.

A saju reading is a product you cannot check before you buy. Until they pay, users cannot know whether the reading is worth the money or whether the price is honest. That hesitation turned straight into drop-off.

02Why the problem mattered

Unless this step was fixed, every extra user brought in would only deepen the loss.

It was the biggest leak

At every other step of the funnel, more than half the users from the step before carried on. Checkout button click-through was only 13%. For the same effort, this was the spot that could move most.

Ads could not fill the gap

Bringing in one user through ads cost two to three times what that user brought in. Spending more on ads with the checkout flow unchanged would grow the loss faster than the revenue.

Where money changes hands, trust comes first

The less a user can see of a product before buying, the more they look for honesty in the price and the tone. I judged that a price dressed up as a discount, or words that rush people to pay, might lift clicks today but would wear trust down. Every screen where money changes hands has the same problem.

So the order was set: fix the checkout flow before spending more on ads. On 22 October I cut the ads instead.

03How I went about it, and what I chose

I decided what to watch first, and wrote it down before changing anything.

Weekly revenue= New visits× Conversion rate× Order value× Orders per buyer

Each week I compared the four boxes with the week before and pushed first on the one that could move most. This case is about the bold box, conversion rate, and within it the checkout button click.

Choice 1

One decision metric: revenue per checkout visitor

Watch conversion alone and you can win by selling cheap. Watch order value alone and you can win by selling less. I made the call on one number that holds both.

Choice 2

Guardrails: conversion rate and confirmed contacts

Even if the decision metric rises, a test is switched off when a guardrail falls further than its set margin. It is the line that stops me losing users while chasing revenue.

Choice 3

Write it down before switching on, review it every week

Before switching a test on, I wrote the hypothesis, the numbers to watch, the decision date and the stop criteria in the change log. Every Monday I grouped the week into what changed, what happened, what I learned and what comes next.

Choice 4

With small samples, roll out only when every signal agrees

For a small service, statistical certainty took weeks. I rolled a change out to everyone only when the decision metric and the guardrails all pointed the same way, and kept watching after that.

Choice 5

People decide, AI agents run

I decided prices, new paid channels and budget increases. AI agents handled screen changes, experiment allocation and result calculations, logging each one in the change log. Screens where money is at stake I approved from captures before they changed.

Designing the checkout screen

  1. What you get, firstThe titles of the four reading chapters appear as a contents list. Users know what they will get before they see the price.
  2. Prices as they areThe first-time price and the price from the second time on sit side by side. No list price that has never actually been charged.
  3. Payment methods at equal sizeKakaoPay, Naver Pay and card sit side by side at the same size. Payment goes straight through, with no login.
  4. Promises right under the buttonOne line under the checkout button covers refunds for unused consultation passes and refunds for errors.

A/B tests on the checkout screen

Showing the price truthfully

The struck-through list price had never actually been charged. Instead of dressing it up as a discount, I showed the first-time price and the price from the second time on, as they really were. The hypothesis was "telling the truth will not sell less".

05What I learned

Five things I take from these three weeks

  1. 01

    Where money changes hands, the truth converts

    The checkout used to show a struck-through list price that had never actually been charged. When I removed it and showed the price as it really was, revenue per checkout visitor rose by 61%. Since then, "a price that has never actually been charged is never used as a comparison price" has been the price display rule.

  2. 02

    A winning screen is not a winning business

    Most of the checkout A/B tests won, but October revenue against ad spend was 67%, short of the 148% break-even. I cut the ads, which brought the daily loss down by 76%, and changed the order so the funnel gets fixed first.

  3. 03

    A win on a small sample gets checked again after rollout

    The bundle of three as the default led by 78% per user in the A/B test, but once it went out to everyone, checkout button click-through halved, so I reverted it. In week three, checkout button click-through fell from 12% to 9%. A screen that won can lose again when the users change.

  4. 04

    One test per spot at a time, with the criteria set before it starts

    On the same day I switched on a price test and a higher-priced product card on the checkout screen. Revenue per checkout visitor fell by 72%, and I could not tell which one caused it. Since then, any spot where money is at stake runs a single experiment, and other changes wait until the call is made.

  5. 05

    Give before you ask

    Instead of asking for an email first, I offered the next two lines right after the point users most wanted to read on, and conversion rose from 5.8% to 9.2%. The step that collects contact details helped payment instead of blocking it.
