Kai's Greedy LoopTuned by a bot, then rebuilt for people

A one-finger arcade roguelite that I design and build at my studio, with AI agents writing all of its code. A bot with perfect reflexes balanced it to a median run of 220 seconds. On the real leaderboard, people's median run was 21 to 26 seconds. So I gave the bot a person's limits, judged each build on its own runs, and rebuilt the first minute so the game's two moves are learnt by doing them.

About the project

Kai's Greedy Loop is a one-finger arcade roguelite. A small creature runs around a loop: tap to change lane; hold, then let go, to lock in what you have eaten. That is the whole rule. There are 64 creatures to collect, each with seven stages of evolution, and six worlds, each with its own palette, joined by gates.

I started it on 22 August 2026. It is past 1,200 commits, and I have not typed a line of its code. AI agents write the code and run the builds and checks; I look at the screen, because they cannot, and say exactly what is wrong. On day one the agents also built a bot that plays the game thousands of times, and the game was balanced with it.

00Product

One finger, two moves.

The goal on day one was one rule, no two runs alike, and a game you could play for years. Tap to change lane. Hold, then let go, to lock in what you have eaten. Everything else in the game has to be read from that one finger: dodge the blocks, carry the creatures, decide how long to stay greedy before you bank.

The first build was a flat 2D circle. Today the same rule runs in 3D across six worlds, and every gate is a door between two worlds that look nothing alike.

dopaplay.io/en/greedyloop plays it in the browser.

Six worlds, one rule

Every gate drops Kai into a world that looks nothing like the one before. Only the first road keeps still; every later one bends, breathes or crosses itself, and each world brings its own new trouble.

One rule of mine holds them together: a thing the player already knows may not quietly behave differently. If its behaviour changes, its form changes too, so a faster wall has to look like a different wall.

Each world has its own page at greedyloop.com/worlds.

01The player problem I set out to solve

The bot lasted 220 seconds. People lasted 22.

Every balance target in the game was a number from a bot with perfect reactions, and the bot was on target. Then I read the real leaderboard and wrote to the agents: "A person playing and you playing are different."

02Why the problem mattered

A target met by a bot said nothing about the game a person gets.

What I built was not being played

Most of the worlds, creatures and events in the game sat beyond a point that almost nobody but me reached. Every week spent on later content was a week spent on something unseen.

The first gate was the bottleneck

Lowering the thresholds later in a run did not change anything, because people never got there. Surviving the first gate did. The first minute was the whole problem.

People skipped the second move

Many players went through without ever learning to hold and release. Without the release, nothing you eat is locked in, so they were playing half a game.

So the order was set: get a person through the first minute before tuning anything that comes after it.

03How I went about it, and what I chose

I changed the instruments first, then the first minute.

Choice 1

Give the bot a person's limits

The agents added profiles to the bot, each with a reaction time, a timing error and the odd late move: perfect, human (0.25 seconds) and slow (0.4 seconds). A sweep plays each profile and prints the share of runs that pass each gate.

Choice 2

Judge the build, not the history

Every posted run carries its build number, what killed it, how far it got, and whether it came from my own test browser, so my test runs sit apart from my real ones.

Choice 3

Teach on the real road

My direction to the agents: pace it so people understand, and no separate difficulty setting. There is no tutorial screen. A first run opens on the lesson, with a real block, a real hoard and a real release.

Choice 4

Nothing lethal, nobody trapped

Nothing can kill you during the lesson. Each beat has a fail-safe in real seconds, and a block you bump gets one more try, so a beginner can get every beat wrong and still be running.

Choice 5

Cut what the player has to think about

On day eight the game had 18 mutations, 8 fusions, turrets and mystery boxes, all working. I wrote: "All that matters in play is dodging well." Two days later they were gone.

The first minute, rebuilt as three beats

1. Tap

A block sits dead ahead in your lane. The world slows to a third, an X stands on the blocked lane and a ring on the way past, and the bubble over Kai says the verb. A tap out of the lane answers it.

2. Hold, then release

A line of creatures in your lane. With three carried and the road clear, the bubble says HOLD and a ring fills under your thumb, then it says RELEASE. Letting go dashes and frees the creatures.

3. Break a block

A block ahead in your lane. The lesson asks for the hold well before it, and says RELEASE only once the block is in the dash's reach. The dash breaks it, and a bounce or a missed block gets one more go.

Each success cheers in the bubble and names the move: THAT'S HOW YOU CHANGE LANE, FREE THEM, BREAK A BLOCK. HOW TO PLAY can replay the lesson at any time.

When people still skipped the hold

Five days later, players were still getting through without learning the hold. So the hold became something you can only learn by doing it.

The lesson waits on the hold, with a ring under your thumb that shows HOLD, then RELEASE. A lesson that ends without a release opens the next run on that beat. Until a player's first release, an ordinary run suggests it whenever three creatures are carried. The first release is called out, YOU'VE GOT IT!, in 12 languages.

Art direction in one line

On day two, image models gave me four looks in a day: glossy with thick outlines, flat vector, big sticker eyes and a pink glow from nowhere. I binned all four. Each piece had been styled on its own, and nothing agreed where the light came from.

The brief I wrote was one line: "The whole thing has to be beautiful." The agents measured the screen before painting anything. Contrast was fine; the whole world was one colour. It needed light, so that same day the game moved to 3D.

The same method on my other games

POPCHAIN: a target before the tuning

Each of the 300 levels is designed with a target win rate for a casual player first. Bots at three reaction speeds, from 0.55 to 1.5 seconds, play every level, and the tuner adjusts the level until the casual win rate fits.

DOPA DASH: the bots caught a wrong floor

A normal bot was clearing the HARD levels over 95% of the time against a 50 to 62% target. A rule that even a beginner must clear 15 to 20% was binding first. I lowered it and retuned 60 levels: the beginner's pass rate on hard levels fell from about 25% to 6 to 19%. I stopped there, because lower would make walls for beginners.

DOPA DASH: a rule I could not read

Playing it myself, I could not tell how to score PERFECT. So the drill moved onto the real field, with a timing bar under the beat and a calibration that corrects a device's sound delay, as with Bluetooth earphones.

05What I learned

Five lessons from tuning a game with bots and people

  1. 01

    A bot that plays your game is not a player

    The perfect bot answered whether a run could be won. It could not answer whether a person would get it. It hit its target while people lasted about a tenth as long.

  2. 02

    Give AI a person's limits before trusting its numbers

    A bot with a late reaction, a timing error and the odd late move was the only instrument that could ask the question that mattered: does a person get past the first gate.

  3. 03

    Teach a move by having people do it

    The lesson uses a real block, a real hoard and a real release, with nothing that can kill you. Each success names the move that was just made, so the first minute is a run of small wins, not a list of orders.

  4. 04

    Cutting is the designer's call

    In one week the agents built 18 mutations and 8 fusions, and all of them worked. Taking them out made the game better. The code can be handed to AI; what stays in the game cannot.

  5. 05

    Judge the build in front of you

    A board that mixes four weeks of a game that changed every day can show that people last far less long than the bot, but not whether today's build is better. Every run now carries its build, and the next step is enough people per build to call it.
